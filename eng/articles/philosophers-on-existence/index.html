Where Did We Come From? Philosophers on the Moment of Birth

A journey through the deepest questions about existence and arrival

3 min read

Every philosophical tradition eventually arrives at the same doorway: something that did not exist now exists, and is aware. Birth is where metaphysics stops being abstract.

Before philosophy: myth and arrival

Long before anyone argued about being, cultures told stories about it. Souls descended from stars, were drawn from rivers, were breathed into clay. What the myths share is an intuition that the newborn came from somewhere rather than simply beginning — that arrival is a journey's end, not a first cause.

Plato: birth as forgetting

Plato gave that intuition its most influential form. The soul, in his account, existed before the body and contemplated the eternal Forms directly. Birth is a descent into matter, and with it comes forgetting. What we call learning is therefore anamnesis — recollection of what the soul already knew.

The idea is philosophically demanding and empirically unprovable, but it explains something real: the strange familiarity of certain truths when we first meet them, the sense of recognising rather than acquiring.

Aristotle: birth as potential

Aristotle, Plato's student, refused the descent. For him the soul is not a passenger in the body but its form — the organising principle that makes this matter a living thing rather than a heap.

Birth, on this view, is not arrival from elsewhere. It is the actualisation of a potential: the acorn becoming the oak. The newborn is not a diminished adult but a complete instance of a being whose nature is to unfold over time.

The Stoics: birth as assignment

The Stoics moved the question from origin to duty. Epictetus compared life to a role in a play: you do not choose the part, only how well you perform it. Marcus Aurelius, writing privately at the edge of an empire, kept returning to the same discipline — you did not select your century, your parents, or your constitution; you selected none of the circumstances and all of the responses.

The Islamic view: fitrah and trust

Islamic thought approaches birth through two ideas. The first is fitrah — the innate disposition every human is born with, oriented towards recognising its Creator before culture writes over it. The second is amanah, trust: existence is given, not owned, and what is given is accounted for.

Together they produce a distinctive position. The newborn is neither a blank surface nor a fallen soul, but a being who arrives already inclined towards truth and already entrusted with a life.

Modern existentialism

Twentieth-century existentialism named the condition sharply. Heidegger called it thrownness: we find ourselves already here, in a situation we did not author. Sartre pressed further — existence precedes essence, so nothing determines what a human being is until that human being acts.

The mood is bleaker than the ancients', but the observation is the same one Plato started from: we begin in the middle of something.

What follows practically

These positions disagree profoundly about where we came from. They converge on something else: none of them treats the circumstances of birth as a verdict. Plato's soul must recollect, Aristotle's potential must be actualised, the Stoic's role must be played, the Muslim's trust must be discharged, the existentialist's freedom must be exercised.

In every case the work belongs to the person, not to the moment of arrival.

The philosophers argue about where the journey began. They agree, unexpectedly, that where it began settles very little about where it goes.
